# Orionid Meteor Shower 2026: Peak Dates and How to Watch from Indonesia

Every October the night sky puts on a free show that needs no telescope: the Orionid meteor shower. The Orionid meteor shower 2026 is expected to peak around 21–22 October. There is one catch to know before you stay up late, which is fairly bright moonlight. With the right preparation, you can still catch some meteors streaking past.

## What the Orionids Are

A meteor shower happens when Earth passes through a trail of dust left behind by a comet. The Orionids come from debris of Halley's Comet. The grains are tiny, often the size of a grain of sand, and burn up completely as they hit the atmosphere at high speed. The light we see is atmospheric gas glowing, not a rock falling to the ground.

They are called Orionids because the meteors appear to radiate from a point in the constellation Orion, known as the radiant. Meteors can show up anywhere in the sky, not only near Orion. In fact, the streaks tend to look longer if you look at an area somewhat away from the radiant.

## Schedule and Peak for 2026

The Orionids stay active for several weeks, from around late September or early October into November. The peak is expected on the night of 21–22 October 2026. Some sources give dates a day apart, so it makes sense to watch for two or three nights around then.

Under ideal sky conditions, the Orionids are usually estimated at around 20 meteors per hour at the peak. That figure assumes a very dark sky with the radiant high overhead. In practice the number you see is often lower, especially with light pollution or a bright moon.

## How Moonlight Affects 2026

The moon is heading toward full this year and is about 72 percent illuminated on the peak night, according to International Meteor Organization estimates quoted by several astronomy sites. Strong light hides faint meteors, so only the brighter ones will register to the eye.

For that reason, the best window is the early morning hours until just before dawn, when the moon is low or has set and Orion's radiant has climbed high. Moonrise and moonset times differ from city to city, so check an astronomy app or BRIN information for your location.

## How to Watch from Indonesia

1. **Find a dark, open spot.** A beach, highlands, or a rural area with a wide view of the sky is far better than the city center. Avoid places with street lamps and tall buildings.
2. **Let your eyes adapt.** It takes about 20 to 30 minutes for your eyes to get used to the dark. Do not look at a bright phone screen. If needed, use a flashlight with a red filter.
3. **Lie back and take in the whole sky.** You do not need a telescope or binoculars. They actually narrow your field of view. Bring a mat or folding chair so waiting is comfortable.
4. **Aim your gaze slightly away from Orion.** Orion's radiant rises during the night, roughly before midnight, and climbs higher toward morning.
5. **Be patient and bring company.** Meteors arrive irregularly. Sometimes several appear close together, then there is a quiet stretch of minutes.
6. **Check the weather.** Clouds block the sky completely. Follow BMKG forecasts for your area and have a backup location.

## Useful Gear

- A light jacket or blanket, since early mornings feel cold in the highlands.
- Warm drinks and snacks.
- Insect repellent if you are near vegetation or water.
- A star map app to locate Orion.
- A spare phone battery.

## Staying Safe While Watching

Choose a safe and busy place, especially when heading out at night. Tell family where you are going and when you will return. Avoid cliffs or dark, empty roads, and do not watch alone in a remote spot. For children, make sure an adult goes along.

## Can You Photograph It with a Phone?

Photographing meteors is hard because they appear at random. The most workable approach is to mount your phone on a tripod, use night mode or a long exposure, point it at a wide patch of sky, and take many shots in a row. Results are not guaranteed, so do not get so busy with the screen that you miss the real show.

## Closing

The 2026 Orionids will not be as spectacular as in a year with no moon, but they are still worth waiting for. Mark 21–22 October, pick a dark place, and plan to watch in the early morning when the moon is low. One or two bright meteors streaking past are enough to make the night memorable. Bring family or friends to make it more fun.
