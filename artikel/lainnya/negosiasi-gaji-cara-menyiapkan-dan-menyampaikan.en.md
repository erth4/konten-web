# Salary Negotiation: How to Prepare and Make Your Case with Confidence

Many people accept the first offer without asking, afraid of seeming greedy or worried the offer will be withdrawn. Yet salary negotiation is a normal part of hiring, and companies usually leave room for it. A small gap in starting pay also has a long reach, because later raises are often calculated from that base figure. With good preparation, you can negotiate politely and still protect the relationship.

## Why Negotiating Is Worth It

A company that has chosen you has already spent time and money recruiting. It wants its chosen candidate to say yes. That puts you in a stronger position at the offer stage than when you applied. Asking politely is not rude; what carries risk is demanding without grounds or phrasing it as an ultimatum.

## Step 1: Research the Market Range

Before naming any figure, know the market value for your role, level of experience, and location. Some sources you can use:

- salary surveys published by recruitment firms,
- pay information on job sites or professional communities,
- conversations with people in a similar field,
- the pay range stated in the job posting, if there is one.

Treat all numbers as estimates, because company size, industry, and city make a big difference. Gather several sources and look for a range that is consistent.

## Step 2: Set Three Numbers

Before negotiating, fix three numbers for yourself:

1. **Your floor.** The lowest figure you would still accept, given living costs and financial obligations.
2. **Your target.** The figure you consider fair based on research and the value you bring.
3. **Your opening number.** Slightly above the target, so you have room to move during the back and forth.

Setting a floor in advance keeps you from accepting an offer you later regret.

## Step 3: Prepare Value-Based Reasons

Companies weigh contribution, not personal need. A reason like "I have many installments" rarely works. Stronger reasons focus on value: highly relevant experience, scarce skills, measurable achievements, or certifications that support the job. Prepare two or three short points, each with an example from previous work.

## Step 4: Choose the Right Moment

The best time to discuss pay is when the company has said it wants to hire you, at the offer stage. If you are asked about salary expectations early on, you can give a wide range or ask to hear the available range first. For current employees seeking a raise, pick a performance review or the end of a successful project, not a moment when the company is struggling.

## Step 5: Say It in Calm Wording

Tone matters as much as the number. Start with appreciation, then make the request. A few examples:

- "Thank you for the offer, I'm very happy with the role. Based on my experience in this field and the market range I've researched, is there room to adjust the salary to around this figure?"
- "I'm very keen to join. Is this offer open for discussion?"

After naming a figure, stop talking and give the other side time to respond. Do not rush to fill the silence by lowering your own request.

## Do Not Look Only at Base Pay

A compensation package is more than a monthly number. If base pay is hard to raise, consider other components:

- meal, transport, or communication allowances,
- annual bonuses or performance incentives,
- health insurance and family benefits,
- leave, flexible hours, or remote work options,
- a training and development budget,
- an earlier salary review, for instance after six months.

Make sure every agreement is written in the offer letter or contract, not just spoken.

## If the Answer Is No

Not every negotiation succeeds, and that is normal. If the company cannot raise the figure, ask whether additional benefits or a review schedule can be discussed. Then decide calmly whether the offer still clears your floor. Stay polite whatever the outcome, because a good reputation travels well beyond a single company.

## Common Mistakes

- Naming the first figure with no research at all.
- Comparing yourself directly and emotionally with coworkers.
- Threatening to leave when you have no intention of doing so.
- Accepting on the spot without asking for time to think. It is fine to ask for a day or two.

## Closing

Salary negotiation is a skill you can practice. Start with market research, set your three numbers, and prepare two value-based reasons. Rehearse the wording with a friend so it sounds natural. With a polite, data-backed approach, you are far more likely to get a fairer offer than if you stay silent.
