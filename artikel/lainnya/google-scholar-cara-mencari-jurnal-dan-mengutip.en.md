# Google Scholar: How to Find Journal Articles and Cite Sources Properly

Typing your thesis topic into regular Google brings up blogs, news, and videos. Typing it into **Google Scholar** brings up papers, books, and theses. That difference shapes the quality of your assignment. "Google scholar" scores an interest of about 6 in Indonesian Google Trends data for October 2025 to October 2026, down about 30 percent on the previous period, even though the tool is still very useful for students and anyone who needs sources they can stand behind.

A note: layouts and menu names can change. Hold on to the method, not the button labels.

## What You're Searching Here

Google Scholar searches scholarly literature: journal articles, conference papers, theses, books, and reports. Not everything is free and not everything has been peer reviewed. Treat Scholar as a doorway, not a quality guarantee. Whether a source is fit to use is still your call.

## Keywords That Work

Start with specific keywords, not long sentences. "The effect of social media on university students' academic achievement" is too long and tends to return scattershot results. Try "social media academic performance university students".

A few habits that improve results:

- **Quotation marks** for phrases that must match exactly, such as "adolescent mental health".
- **Two languages.** Search in your own language, then in English with equivalent terms. International literature is usually larger.
- **Advanced search.** Use it to narrow by author, journal name, or year range.
- **Year range.** The filter on the left helps find recent research, but don't ignore classic works that get cited often.
- **Synonyms.** One concept has several names. Try a few combinations.

## Reading the Results

Each result shows a title, authors, journal or publisher, year, and a snippet. Below it are usually several useful links:

- **Cited by.** Shows other works that cite this paper. Besides hinting at its influence, it's a quick way to find newer research on the same topic.
- **Related articles.** A list of papers considered similar.
- **All versions.** The same paper is sometimes available in several places, including a university repository or the author's own site.
- **Save.** Puts the paper in your personal library.
- **Cite.** Opens a window with ready-made citations.

Be careful about judging quality from the citation count alone. A high number signals attention, not necessarily accuracy. A paper many people criticize can still be cited a lot.

## Getting the Full Text

On the right side of a result, a link labelled PDF or HTML leads to text you can read directly. If there isn't one, a few things are worth trying:

1. Click **All versions** and see whether an open copy exists.
2. Search the exact title in a university or publisher repository.
3. Link your institution's library access through the Library Links setting in Scholar, if your school or university subscribes to journals. That widens the articles you can open.
4. Contact the library. They can often help track it down.
5. Consider politely emailing the author. Many are glad to share their papers.

Use only legitimate routes. Downloading from piracy sites breaks copyright and puts your device at risk.

## Citing: The Button Helps, but Don't Trust It Blindly

The Cite button opens a window with citations in several styles, such as APA and MLA, plus an option to export to a reference manager. It saves a lot of time. But automatically generated citations are sometimes wrong: capitalization, author names, page numbers, or publisher details can be off because the source data isn't uniform.

So:

1. Copy the citation from Scholar as a draft.
2. Compare it with the title page or information page of the original paper.
3. Adjust it to the style guide your university or journal requires.
4. Make sure every source in your text appears in the reference list, and the other way round.

Free and paid reference managers can help keep things consistent, but final responsibility stays with you.

## Saving and Following

- **Personal library.** Save relevant papers and group them with labels.
- **Email alerts.** Set an alert for a topic or author, and you'll be told when a new paper appears.
- **Notes.** Write down why you saved each paper. Two months later you'll thank yourself.

## Judging Whether a Source Is Fit to Use

Scholar's results are mixed. A few questions to filter by:

- Is it published in a clear, reputable journal? Check the publisher and its peer-review policy.
- Are the authors and their affiliations clear?
- Is the method explained and the data defensible?
- Have there been criticisms or corrections?
- Does the journal look like it publishes anything for a fee? Beware predatory journals. Ask a lecturer or librarian if unsure.

For domestic literature, national reference portals such as Garuda and indexes such as SINTA can be good complements. Check their availability and features directly on their sites.

## Tidy Research Habits

1. Write your research question first, then choose keywords.
2. Record the keywords and the date of each search so you can repeat it.
3. Read the abstract first; don't copy straight from the snippet.
4. Follow the citation chain: from one good paper, see whom it cites and who cites it.
5. Note sources while reading, not at the end.

Try it now: take one paper you've already used, click Cited by, and look at the three newest papers there. You might find a source stronger than the one in your hands.
