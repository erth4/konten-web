# Job Scams: How to Spot Fake Job Offers and Avoid Them

Job hunting is when people are at their most vulnerable: they need income, they are anxious while waiting for news, and they tend to trust offers that look promising. Scammers understand this. They post fake openings on social media, in chat groups, or on sites that imitate real job portals, then push applicants to pay money or hand over personal data. The good news is that most of these schemes follow patterns you can learn to recognize.

## The Most Common Schemes

**Upfront fees.** Applicants are asked to pay for "administration", "uniforms", "training", "a medical test", or "a deposit" before they start. Legitimate employers generally do not charge applicants for the hiring process.

**Data harvesting.** The application form asks for a photo of your ID, a selfie holding the ID, your bank account number, or a one-time code. This data can be used to take out loans in your name or break into your accounts.

**Part-time jobs that are too easy.** Tasks such as liking videos, writing reviews, or "locking in" orders for quick pay. At first victims receive small payouts to build trust, then are asked to deposit growing sums that never come back.

**Overseas placement outside official channels.** Big salaries and fast departure, but no clear contract. In the worst cases this leads to human trafficking or forced labor.

**Borrowed company names.** Scammers copy the logo and name of a well-known employer, then contact applicants through personal chat accounts rather than the company's official email.

## Warning Signs

- A salary far above market for a job with very light requirements.
- A hiring process that is too fast: hired on the spot with no proper interview.
- Interviews held only through a messaging app, with no in-person meeting or official video call.
- The sender's email uses a free domain, or a lookalike domain where one letter differs from the real company name.
- Messages full of typos, odd phrasing, or pressure to decide the same day.
- A vague office address, or one that does not match official sources.
- You are asked to keep the offer secret from family or friends.

One sign alone does not prove a scam, but two or three together are a strong reason to stop and check.

## How to Verify an Opening

1. **Visit the company's official website** by typing the address yourself, not by following a link in a message. See whether the job appears on their careers page.
2. **Match the contact details.** Call or email the contact listed on the official site and ask whether the opening is real and the recruiter's name checks out.
3. **Check the recruiter's profile** on a professional network: is the account old, does it have a believable work history, and is it connected to the company's employees?
4. **Search the company name with "scam" or "reviews"** to see what other applicants experienced.
5. **Ask specific questions** about the role, the manager, and daily tasks. A real recruiter can answer in detail, while a scammer tends to dodge.

## Protect Your Personal Data

Before an interview and a signed contract, sharing your CV is enough. Documents such as your ID, family card, and bank account number only become reasonable to request after a clear, official offer. Cover details that are not needed on copies of documents, and add a watermark stating the intended use. Never give a one-time code, PIN, or password to anyone, including someone who claims to be a recruiter.

## If You Have Already Been Targeted

- **Stop communicating and do not send more money**, even if you are promised a refund.
- **Keep evidence:** screenshots of chats, proof of transfer, account numbers, and the link to the posting.
- **Contact your bank** or e-wallet provider to report the transaction and ask that the recipient account be reviewed.
- **Secure your accounts:** change passwords, turn on two-step verification, and watch for loans or new accounts opened in your name.
- **Report it** to the police and to official complaint channels, and to the platform where the posting appeared so the account can be acted on.

Do not feel ashamed. These scams are designed carefully, and victims come from all walks of life.

## Closing

The simple rule: a legitimate job does not ask you to pay in order to work, and does not ask for your account secrets. Whenever an offer feels too good, spend five minutes verifying it through official channels before replying. Share this with friends who are job hunting, because one warning message is often enough to prevent a large loss.
