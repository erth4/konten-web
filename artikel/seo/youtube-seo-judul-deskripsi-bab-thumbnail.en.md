# YouTube SEO: How to Optimize Titles, Descriptions, and Video Chapters

"Youtube" scores an interest of 83 in Indonesian Google Trends data (October 2025 to October 2026), with "yt" following at 36. Don't be fooled. Most people typing that word just want to open YouTube, not find your video. That's the starting point for talking about **YouTube SEO**.

The opportunity sits somewhere else: the search box inside YouTube. There, people type very specific questions, like how to swap a motorbike tyre or whether a phone is worth buying. Videos that answer questions like that have a real shot, and **YouTube SEO** is about making sure yours doesn't sink among thousands of others.

One confession up front. YouTube's recommendation formula isn't public and keeps changing, so anyone promising a "guaranteed growth trick" is selling hope. What you can control is how clearly your video is understood, by viewers and by the system alike.

## Found Through Search or Through Recommendations

These two routes behave differently.

Through search, viewers arrive with keywords in mind. Titles, descriptions, and chapters matter most here.

Through recommendations, a video appears on the home page or beside another video based on viewing habits. Here the pull of the thumbnail and the video's ability to hold people to the end count for more.

Videos that last tend to do well at both: the topic is searched for, and the content keeps people watching.

## Start With the Question, Not the Camera

Before recording, eavesdrop on what viewers are asking.

- Type your topic into YouTube's search box and watch the autocomplete. Those are phrases people really do type.
- Watch a few top videos on the same topic. What did they skip? What do the comments complain about?
- Check Google Trends to see whether interest in the topic is climbing or fading.
- Read the comments on your own videos. Repeated questions are your next video list, free of charge.

## Titles: Clear First, Catchy Second

A good title answers two things at once: what is this about, and why should I watch.

"Motorbike Tyre" answers nothing. "How to Change a Motorbike Tyre Yourself in 10 Minutes" answers both.

Put the main keyword near the front so it doesn't get cut off on a phone screen. Around 60 characters is a safe guide, not a law. Don't write it all in capitals, and don't promise something the video doesn't deliver. A viewer who feels cheated closes the video within seconds, and that does more damage than a dull title ever will.

## Descriptions: The First Two Lines Carry the Weight

The start of the description appears in search results and before the "show more" button. Use it to explain the video in ordinary sentences containing the main keyword, then continue with a summary of the points, links to related videos or playlists, any sources you mention, and the chapter list.

What you don't need: a pile of keywords with no sentences around them. It helps nobody and looks like spam.

## Video Chapters

Chapters break a video into sections people can jump to. A viewer who only needs step three shouldn't have to sit through ten minutes of intro.

According to YouTube's guidance, chapters are created through a list of timestamps in the description. The officially listed requirements: the first timestamp must be 00:00, and there must be at least three timestamps in ascending order. Some other sources add that each chapter must run at least 10 seconds. Since rules can shift, check YouTube's help page before you upload.

Like this:

- 00:00 Introduction
- 01:20 What you'll need
- 03:45 Step one
- 07:10 Mistakes people often make

Give them names that explain, using the terms people search for. "Part 2" tells nobody anything.

## Thumbnails

A thumbnail is a one-second ad for your video. One clear object, short high-contrast text, and still readable when shrunk to the size of a postage stamp. Keep the style consistent across the channel so people recognize your videos before reading the title.

And be honest. A misleading thumbnail can lift clicks today but drags down viewer satisfaction, which ends up hurting the video itself.

## Captions and Translations

Captions help viewers who can't listen, who are learning a language, or who watch on mute on public transport. Check the automatic version, because technical terms and people's names often come out wrong. If your audience speaks another language, adding translated titles and descriptions is worth trying too.

## The Channel as a Whole

Group videos on one subject into playlists so viewers watch in sequence. Use cards and end screens to point to the next relevant video. Say in the channel description what the channel is about and who it's for. On schedule, being realistic and steady beats posting three videos a week for a month and then vanishing.

## Read the Data

Channel analytics tell you where viewers come from, how long they stay, and at which minute they leave. If lots of people bail in the first ten seconds, your intro runs too long. If one video does well through search, make a follow-up on that topic before someone else does.

Finally, the avoid list: misleading titles and thumbnails, copying other people's videos, cramming in irrelevant keywords or hashtags, and buying fake viewers. All of them look like shortcuts, and all of them lead nowhere.

Start with one video. Rewrite the title, tidy the first two lines of the description, add chapters. Then see what changes over a month.
