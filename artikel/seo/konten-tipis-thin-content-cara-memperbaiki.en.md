# Thin Content: How to Spot It and How to Fix It

The temptation is obvious: more pages, more chances. So short articles keep getting added, a category is created for a single post, and a few near-twin articles appear because the keyword was "slightly different." A year later the site has hundreds of pages and traffic has stalled. There's a term for this symptom: **thin content**.

The problem may not be too few pages. It may be too many pages that help nobody.

One thing to clear up: thin doesn't mean short. A 200-word page that fully answers a question can be more useful than a rambling 2,000-word article. The yardstick is value to the reader, not word count.

## What Thin Content Looks Like

The signs are fairly consistent.

- The page skims the topic with no explanation or steps anyone can use.
- The text is lifted from elsewhere, or hundreds of pages differ only by a city name.
- Category, tag, or internal search pages that are nearly empty.
- Text mass-produced automatically with no editing and no knowledge behind it.
- Pages whose only job is to send visitors on to someone else.
- Generic sentences you could paste onto any topic without changing the meaning.

Google's spam guidance names pages with little or no added value as a problem, especially when produced in bulk to catch searches. How this is judged can change, so refer to Google Search Central's official documentation for the latest.

## Why It Hurts

Readers who can't find an answer head straight back to the results, and that repeats across lots of pages. A large number of weak pages also blurs your good ones. Search engines waste time crawling pages that serve no purpose. And if the pattern of low-value content is deliberate, a site can face manual action.

There's no magic number for how many thin pages is "too many." What's certain is that the overall quality of a site influences how it's judged.

## How to Audit

It's dull work, but the payoff is real.

1. List every page. A sitemap, an export from your content management system, or a crawler tool will do.
2. Pull performance data. From Search Console get clicks and impressions per page over six to twelve months. From analytics, get visits and time on page.
3. Flag candidates: few words, almost no visits, no inbound links.
4. Read them one by one. Don't just trust the numbers. Ask yourself: if I'd stumbled onto this page, would I feel helped?
5. Group by problem: duplication, overlapping topics, empty, or outdated.

Repeat every six months or a year. An audit done once in a lifetime isn't worth much.

## Four Decisions for Each Page

**Deepen.** If the topic is still relevant and people search for it, fill it with something genuinely new: step-by-step explanation, real examples, your own data, images, answers to follow-up questions. Adding words without adding value only makes a thin page long.

**Merge.** If several pages cover nearly the same thing, make them one complete page. Redirect the old addresses to the merged page with a 301 redirect, then update internal links. Signals that were scattered come together.

**Redirect or remove.** If a page has no value and no counterpart, just delete it. If a relevant page exists as a replacement, redirect there. If no fitting replacement exists, let it return a not-found status. Don't send everything to the home page, since that only confuses readers.

**Keep it, but hide it from the index.** Some pages help users but mean little in search results, like internal search results, thin tag pages, or filter pages. Consider marking them noindex.

## A Concrete Example (Made Up, but Common)

A shoe shop has five articles: "how to choose running shoes", "tips for choosing running shoes", "best running shoes for beginners", "choosing the right running shoes", and "running shoe guide". Each is 250 words with nearly the same content.

The sensible call: merge all five into one thorough guide covering foot shape, sizing, materials, and type of running, then redirect the five old addresses to it. One strong page replaces five weak ones.

## Preventing It at the Writing Stage

Prevention is cheaper than cleanup. Start from the reader's question, not from the keyword alone. Make sure each page has a purpose no other page has. If two planned articles answer the same question, write one. Add something that's hard to copy: first-hand experience, data, examples, a point of view.

If you use AI as a helper, treat its output as a rough draft. Check the facts, add real context, and don't churn out dozens of uniform pieces in one go. That's exactly the recipe for thin content.

## Mistakes When Fixing

Adding filler paragraphs changes nothing. Deleting everything without looking can throw away short pages that were actually useful. After merging, internal links often get forgotten. And redirecting everything to the home page just moves the problem.

Start with the ten pages that get the fewest visits on your site. Read them one by one. Most of the decisions will feel obvious once you've read them.
