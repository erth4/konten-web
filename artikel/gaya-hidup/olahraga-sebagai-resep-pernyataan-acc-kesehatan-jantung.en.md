# Exercise as Prescription: The New ACC Statement on Heart Health

Doctors usually write prescriptions for medicine. What if the prescription read: walk for thirty minutes, five times a week? That's the direction of a new scientific statement from the American College of Cardiology (ACC), published in the journal JACC on 15 October 2026. The core message is simple: **exercise as prescription** deserves to be a central part of routine heart care, not a throwaway line at the end of a visit.

This piece sums up what has been reported, what it means for ordinary people, and how to start without overdoing it. The information is general. If you have heart disease or worrying symptoms, talk to your doctor first.

## What the Statement Says

It's titled Exercise Prescriptions for Cardiovascular Health in Adults: 2026 ACC Scientific Statement. Because I've only read summaries and news coverage, not the full text, here are the points that are consistently reported.

- **Exercise is treated as core care,** not an add-on. Clinicians are encouraged to prescribe it like any other therapy, tailored to the patient's condition.
- **The biggest gains come at the very start.** Moving from inactive to regularly active gives the largest jump. Adding volume and intensity gives further benefit where clinically appropriate.
- **Daily steps.** Data cited suggest roughly 5,000 to 7,000 steps a day are linked to lower risk of death from cardiovascular disease and other causes in many adults.
- **Fitness is a risk factor.** Low cardiorespiratory fitness is described as a marker of future heart risk comparable to smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and diabetes.
- **The prescription must be personal.** It's tailored to a patient's condition, symptoms, physical capacity, goals, and preferences.
- **The headline message** came from the writing committee's chair, Jonathan Myers: some activity is better than none.

## How to Read the 5,000 to 7,000 Step Figure

This number is easy to misread. A few notes:

- It's a **statistical link** across many people, not a guarantee for any one person.
- It isn't a single target. What's highlighted is that this range is already tied to real benefit, so there's no need to wait until you reach 10,000 steps.
- Steps aren't the only measure. Intensity, strength training, and balance matter too.
- If you currently walk 2,000 steps a day, a realistic aim is to add a little at a time, not leap to a daunting figure.

## Why the Message Matters

Many people treat exercise as a lifestyle choice. This statement shifts that view: fitness counts as much as blood pressure or cholesterol numbers. If low fitness really is comparable as a risk factor, checking it and improving it deserves a place in the conversation with your doctor before something goes wrong, not after.

## Starting Safely

For healthy adults who want to be more active, a few general principles:

1. **Start small.** Add five to ten minutes of walking a day, not a full hour straight away.
2. **Build up slowly.** Add a little duration or pace every week or two.
3. **Choose what you can keep doing.** Walking, cycling, swimming, dancing. The best exercise is the one you don't drop.
4. **Use the talk test.** At moderate intensity you can speak in sentences but can't sing.
5. **Add strength work** a few times a week, with body weight or light weights.
6. **Keep track.** A step count from your phone or watch is enough to see progress.
7. **Schedule it.** Treat it as an appointment, not something you do if there's time.

A commonly quoted general guideline for adults is about 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity a week. It's a benchmark, not an entry requirement. Starting with less is still useful.

## If You Already Have Heart Disease

This is where the word "prescription" does its job properly. Don't design your own exercise programme without consulting anyone, especially if you have heart disease, uncontrolled high blood pressure, diabetes, or symptoms such as chest pain or unusual breathlessness. Ask your doctor:

- What type and intensity of exercise is safe for me?
- Do I need any checks before I start?
- Is there a cardiac rehabilitation programme I can join?
- What signs mean I should stop?

## Signs to Stop and Seek Help

Stop and get medical help if you feel chest pain or pressure, breathlessness out of proportion to the effort, severe dizziness, fainting, or an abnormal racing heartbeat. Don't brush it off just because you're exercising.

## On Motivation

When it starts to feel hard, remember Myers' message: some is better than none. Ten minutes of walking on a bad day isn't wasted. Consistency matters more than perfection.

As a first step, check how many steps you took yesterday. Then set this week's target just slightly higher. That already follows the heart of what the cardiologists said.
