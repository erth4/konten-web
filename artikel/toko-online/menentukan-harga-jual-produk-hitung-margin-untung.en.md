# How to Set Your Selling Price: Work Out Cost, Fees, and Margin

Plenty of new sellers price things the easiest way possible: look at the cost, add ten thousand, done. Orders roll in, everyone's happy. At month end, the bank balance is lower than expected and nobody can say where the money went. That's a sign the way you **set your selling price** needs fixing.

The answer is usually in small costs that never made it into the sum. This article is about how to **set your selling price** without leaning on gut feeling. All the rupiah figures below are illustrations of how to calculate, not market data. Marketplace fees, shipping costs, and deductions in your own case need checking in the seller terms and your shop records.

## Step 1: Work Out Your True Cost

"Cost" isn't just what you paid for the goods. The cost per unit covers everything that gets one item ready to ship:

- purchase price or production cost,
- shipping from your supplier to you,
- packaging: box, bubble wrap, tape, stickers,
- reasonable breakage and shrinkage,
- an allowance for returns, if your business sees them.

Illustrative example: goods bought at Rp40,000, packaging Rp3,000, allowance for breakage and returns Rp2,000. Cost per unit: **Rp45,000**, not Rp40,000. That Rp5,000 gap quietly eats your profit if forgotten.

## Step 2: List the Costs Attached to a Sale

Some costs only appear when an item sells, and they're usually a percentage of the price:

- marketplace commission or service fees,
- payment processing fees,
- commissions for resellers or affiliates,
- advertising spend spread across each sale.

Percentages differ between platforms and can change, so open your seller settings or help centre and note the current figures. In this example we use one hypothetical number: total deductions of 7 percent of the selling price.

## Step 3: Decide the Profit You Want

Decide how much net profit per unit makes the business worth running, after all costs. Think about pay for your own time as well. Sellers who don't count their hours are often startled to find they're working for very small wages.

Say the target profit is Rp10,000 per unit.

## A Formula That Doesn't Fool You

Because marketplace deductions are a percentage of the selling price, adding them as a fixed amount will come out wrong. Use this:

**Selling price = (cost + target profit) ÷ (1 − fee percentage)**

With the example numbers: (45,000 + 10,000) ÷ (1 − 0.07) = 55,000 ÷ 0.93 ≈ **Rp59,140**.

Round to a friendly number, say Rp59,900. Check: Rp59,900 minus 7 percent fees (about Rp4,193) leaves Rp55,707. Subtract the Rp45,000 cost and net profit is about **Rp10,700**. Target met, with a little extra.

## Margin and Markup: Two Things Often Swapped

These two terms get used interchangeably though they differ.

- **Markup** is profit divided by cost. In the example: 10,700 ÷ 45,000 ≈ 24 percent.
- **Margin** is profit divided by selling price. In the example: 10,700 ÷ 59,900 ≈ 18 percent.

If someone says "30 percent profit," ask: of cost or of price? A 30 percent markup works out to a margin of only about 23 percent. Mixing them up can make your profit target miss quietly.

## Discounts Are Expensive

This is the part that tends to shock people. Same example. The normal price of Rp59,900 gives profit of about Rp10,700. Offer a 10 percent discount and the price becomes about Rp53,900. After the 7 percent fee, roughly Rp50,100 is left. Subtract the Rp45,000 cost and profit shrinks to about **Rp5,100**.

A 10 percent discount cuts profit by more than half. To make up the lost profit, sales would have to roughly double. Run the numbers before joining a promo, and ask: will this promo truly double my orders?

## Look at Competitor Prices, but Don't Follow Blindly

Check prices of similar products. It's useful for knowing the reasonable range in the market. But don't cut your price just because someone is cheaper. First ask whether they carry the same costs. A competitor selling below your cost may be clearing stock, may have deep pockets, or may have forgotten to count their costs. Price wars are rarely won by the smallest player.

Another way to compete is to add value: better packaging, clearer photos, faster chat replies, or bundles that lift the buyer's total basket.

## Review on a Schedule

Pricing isn't a one-time decision. Revisit it when your costs rise, shipping rates change, or platform fees are updated. If your product's raw materials come from abroad, exchange-rate shifts can nudge your cost without any announcement. A simple habit: at the start of each month, open your spreadsheet and check whether your three best sellers still earn the profit you aimed for.

## Mistakes That Happen Often

- Not counting packaging and returns.
- Adding marketplace fees as a fixed figure.
- Giving discounts without working out the impact.
- Mixing business money with personal money so profit is invisible.
- Cutting the price without changing anything on the value side.

Take your best-selling product today and recalculate with the formula above. If the result makes you slightly uncomfortable, that's a sign the sum is finally right.
