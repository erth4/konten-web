# Budgeting for Freelancers: Managing Money When Income Isn't Steady

March: nine million comes in and you feel rich. April: three million, and the instalment is due on the fifth. That tidy 50-30-20 budget from every personal finance article suddenly feels like it was written for someone whose pay never changes. It was. That's why **budgeting for freelancers** can't copy the salaried playbook.

A salaried employee receives the same figure every month, so they can split their pay neatly. A freelancer doesn't have that luxury. That's why a **freelancer budget** has to be built on different logic: not dividing a salary, but creating one out of income that rises and falls.

## Don't Start From Income, Start From Spending

With uncertain income, the stable place to begin is what's certain: your expenses.

1. **Work out your minimum living cost.** Rent or mortgage, food, transport, electricity and internet, insurance or health contributions, debt instalments, and other fixed needs. This is your floor: the amount that has to exist every month for life to keep running.
2. **Work out your cost of working.** Software subscriptions, data, shared workspace, equipment. Keep it apart from personal costs.
3. **Add reserves for tax and contributions.** More on that below.

That's the number your income has to chase, not the other way round.

## Find an Honest Average Income

Take data from the last six to twelve months, then work out the average. An illustrative six months: 4, 9, 3, 7, 12, and 5 million rupiah. The total is 40 million, an average of about 6.7 million a month.

Look at the lowest month too. In that example it's 3 million. If your minimum living cost is 5 million, it's plain that some months will have to be covered from reserves.

Don't use your best month as the benchmark. That's the most common temptation, and it often ends in a lifestyle that ordinary months can't support.

## Pay Yourself a Wage: The Trick That Changes Everything

The core idea is simple. All client payments land in one holding account. On a set date, say the 1st, you transfer a fixed "wage" from that account to your personal account, the same amount every month.

From the example, with an average of 6.7 million and a floor of 5 million, you could set the wage at 5 million. In busy months the surplus builds up in the holding account. In slow months that account covers the gap. In your personal account you live like a salaried worker: the monthly budget can be split neatly because the figure is certain.

Raise your wage only once the holding account has several months of reserve.

## Three Pots of Money

To keep things from mixing, separate them into three pots. They can be three accounts, or one account with separate pockets.

- **Holding and reserve.** Where all income lands and the reserve builds.
- **Tax and contributions.** Set aside part of every payment right after it arrives.
- **Personal.** Receives your fixed wage.

Keeping business and personal money apart also helps you see whether your work is actually profitable.

## A Thicker Emergency Fund

An emergency fund matters even more for freelancers, because no salary carries on when a client disappears or you fall ill. Many financial planners suggest a bigger reserve than for salaried staff, for instance six to twelve months of living costs, though the figure depends on your situation. Build it gradually. Start with a one-month target, then three months, and so on. Keep it somewhere easy to withdraw from but not easy to dip into on impulse.

## Tax: Don't Wait Until Year-End

Income from freelance work generally counts as taxable income. There's no automatic deduction like on an employee's pay, so setting aside part of every payment is a habit that saves you. Rules, rates, and reporting methods can change and depend on your situation, so check the Directorate General of Taxes' official site or ask a tax consultant. The key point: don't treat everything in the account as yours.

## Social Security and Insurance

With no office paying contributions, protection is in your hands. Consider voluntary health coverage and, where available, the employment programme for workers who aren't wage recipients. Check the latest requirements with the administering body. Add life or health insurance according to your needs and dependants. The cheapest protection is what you buy before you get sick.

## Managing Client Cash Flow

Part of a freelancer's trouble isn't slow orders but late payments.

- **Ask for a deposit** on big projects or with new clients.
- **Split payment into milestones** by stage of work.
- **Write payment terms and deadlines** into the agreement or invoice.
- **Send the invoice right away** once the work is done, rather than waiting to be asked.
- **Track unpaid bills** and chase politely but consistently.

If some of your income comes in a foreign currency, remember the exchange rate affects what you actually receive. Don't spend all of it as if the rate were certain to hold.

## Facing Slow Months

Slow months will come. Plan ahead: use the holding account, cut non-essential spending first, and use the time to look for new clients or refresh your portfolio. Avoid covering slow months with pay-later or credit cards without a plan to clear them. Consumer debt taken on in a slow month adds pressure to the next one.

## Review Monthly

Spend half an hour at month end: how much came in, how much went out, how much went to reserve. Adjust your own wage every three or six months based on the latest average.

Today, work out your minimum living cost as a single figure. Write it down. That number decides how well you'll sleep during your next slow month.
