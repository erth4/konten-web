# Google Drive: How to Share Files Safely and Control Access

Picture an office admin who drops a spreadsheet of salaries into the staff chat group. To keep things quick, she sets it to "anyone with the link." Six months on, that link is still live, has been forwarded to who knows how many people, and nobody remembers who was supposed to have it. It's a small slip, right up until the day it isn't. That's why the sharing settings in **Google Drive** deserve to be taken seriously.

People do search for this a lot. In Indonesian Google Trends data for October 2025 to October 2026, "drive" is up roughly 20 percent on the prior period. Coursework, reports, and photos travel as links because links are lighter than attachments. What gets overlooked is the setting behind the Share button. In **Google Drive**, two wrong clicks can open a private file to anyone at all.

Menus change whenever Google updates the product, so treat the names below as directions rather than exact quotes.

## Pick the Door First

Each time you press Share, you're choosing between two doors.

**Restricted.** Only people you add by email can open the file, and they generally have to sign in with a Google account. This is the safest default.

**Anyone with the link.** Whoever has the link gets in, and you won't know who they are. Links get copied, forwarded, and left sitting in somebody's chat history.

The second door isn't wrong for material that's meant to be public, like a brochure or a price list. For pay slips, contracts, ID scans, or client data, though, use the first one. Full stop. If your account is run by an employer or a school, an administrator may also limit which options show up.

## Three Roles, Three Levels of Trust

Once you've decided who gets in, decide what they can do.

- **Viewer** can read and nothing else. Good for final reports and reference material.
- **Commenter** can read and leave notes without touching the content. Right for a lecturer marking work or a manager reviewing a draft.
- **Editor** can change the content and, depending on settings, decide who else gets access. Give it only to people actually working on the file with you.

A handy rule of thumb: start at Viewer. Raise it only when someone complains they can't type.

## The Folder Trap

This one claims the most victims. Permissions on a folder flow down to everything inside, including files you add later. So if "Shared Project" is already open to twenty people and you drop a draft contract in there, those twenty people can read it. Keep public and private folders apart from the start, and don't park anything sensitive where many people collaborate.

There's one more quirk. You can't give someone less access to a single file if they already have more through its parent folder.

## A Sharing Routine That Isn't Careless

1. Right-click the file or folder and choose Share.
2. Type recipients' emails one at a time. Check the spelling, because one wrong letter sends the file to a stranger.
3. Set each person's role.
4. Look at the general access section. Make sure it says Restricted unless you really mean to open it up.
5. Send.

For a public link, copy it, then double-check the role attached. A public link set to Editor is a recipe for chaos.

## Review Who Still Has Access

Sharing isn't a one-time decision. Every few months, spend ten minutes going through the files you've shared:

- remove people who are no longer involved, like a colleague who changed teams or the committee of an event that wrapped up,
- turn public links back to Restricted once the material no longer needs to be open,
- for important files, set an access expiry date if your account offers the option.

## Don't Forget the Front Door

Neat sharing settings are worthless if your own Google account gets broken into. Turn on two-step verification, use a password you don't reuse anywhere else, and be suspicious of emails claiming "someone shared a file with you" from senders you don't know. Scammers like to imitate sharing notices to steer victims to a fake sign-in page. Look at the address in the browser bar before typing a password.

One more thing people forget: don't make Drive the only home for important files. A locked account or an accidental deletion can happen to anyone. Keep a copy somewhere else.

Try this today: open the last three files you shared and look at who can reach them. The result may surprise you.
